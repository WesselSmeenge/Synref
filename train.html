FOCUS
Train your attention span
A four-week protocol to rebuild sustained focus from the ground up.

The principle: your attention span is a muscle. It has atrophied from years of low-resistance, high-stimulation input. To rebuild it, you apply progressive overload: start below your current capacity, then increase the demand weekly. Do not skip ahead.

Week 1

Work in 20-minute focused blocks with no interruptions. Phone in another room. One task only. After 20 minutes, take a 5-minute break where you do not look at a screen. Repeat twice per day. The goal this week is simply to build the habit of starting.

Week 2

Extend to 30-minute blocks. Add one block per day so you are doing three per day. If 30 minutes feels too short, that is a good sign. Do not extend yet. Consistency over intensity at this stage.

Week 3

Move to 45-minute blocks. Introduce one deep work session per day of 90 minutes where you do your most cognitively demanding work. This is where you begin to experience what real focus feels like.

Week 4 and beyond

Standard sessions of 50 minutes with 10-minute breaks, following the Pomodoro structure adapted for longer duration. Your target is two to three 90-minute deep work sessions per day as a long-term baseline.

SELF-AWARENESS
Train self-awareness
Learn to notice your own unconscious patterns before they run your behavior.

Most people believe they are self-aware. Research consistently shows they are wrong. Self-awareness is not a trait you either have or do not have. It is a skill that improves with deliberate practice. The starting point is learning to observe yourself without immediately judging what you see.

Practice 1: The pause

Before any significant decision or reaction, pause for ten seconds. Ask yourself: what am I about to do, and why? This single habit interrupts the automatic pilot loop more effectively than almost anything else. It sounds trivial. It is not.

Practice 2: The end-of-day review

Each evening, write answers to three questions. What did I do today that aligned with my values? What did I do that did not? What triggered my worst responses today? Do this for 30 days without skipping. Patterns will emerge that you cannot see in the moment.

Practice 3: Trigger mapping

Choose one behavior you want to change. Each time it happens, note what preceded it. What were you doing, feeling, or thinking immediately before? After two weeks, you will know your triggers with enough precision to design around them.

Practice 4: Seek accurate feedback

Ask two people you trust to tell you one thing you do that undermines your effectiveness. Most people never ask this question. The answers are consistently more useful than months of solo reflection.

SLEEP
Build your sleep habits
A practical protocol for resetting your sleep, one week at a time.

You cannot negotiate with biology. Your circadian rhythm operates on a 24-hour cycle anchored by light and timing. Every inconsistency you introduce costs you cognitive performance the next day. The training here is behavioral, not motivational.

Week 1: Anchor your wake time

Choose a single wake time and commit to it for seven consecutive days including the weekend. Do not adjust it based on how late you went to bed. This is the hardest week. Your body is recalibrating. The sleep quality from day five onward will noticeably improve.

Week 2: Build the wind-down

Add a 45-minute wind-down before your target bedtime. Dim lights, no stimulating content, no social media. The activity should be calm and repeatable: reading, light stretching, journaling. Do the same thing in the same order each night. Your brain learns to associate the sequence with sleep.

Week 3: Optimize the environment

Make your bedroom as dark and cool as possible. Remove all screens from the bedroom. If your phone is your alarm, buy a separate alarm clock. The bedroom should be a place your brain associates only with sleep. This is an environmental intervention, not a behavioral one, and it works passively once set up.

Week 4: Protect your morning

Do not check your phone for the first 30 minutes after waking. Get natural light within the first hour, ideally through a short walk outside. Morning light exposure is the single most powerful signal you can give your circadian rhythm that the day has started. It also anchors your evening tiredness at the right time.
